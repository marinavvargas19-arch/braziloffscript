# Is Brazil Safe for Tourists in 2027? An Honest Local Guide

*What the official advisories say, where the real risks are and how to avoid them.*

::photo rio

Yes, Brazil is safe for tourists who travel with the same street sense they would use in any big city, and most of the roughly nine million foreign visitors who came in 2025 went home with nothing worse than a sunburn. The real risks are specific and avoidable: phone theft, beach snatching, drink spiking and unlicensed taxis in the large cities, not the nature destinations most travelers dream about.

That's the short answer. The longer one depends on where you go, what time you go out and how you move around. Here's what we tell our own guests.

## What do the official travel advisories say?

The US State Department rates Brazil Level 2 on a scale of 1 to 4, which means "Exercise increased caution." It advises against going to three kinds of places:

- areas within 160 km (100 miles) of Brazil's land borders with other South American countries. Iguazu Falls National Park and Pantanal National Park are excluded, so you can visit them normally;
- favelas, even on a guided tour;
- some of Brasília's satellite cities (Ceilândia, Santa Maria, São Sebastião and Paranoá), at night.

Canada asks travelers to "exercise a high degree of caution." The UK Foreign Office updated its Brazil page on November 17, 2026, with practical warnings about robbery, phone theft and drink spiking.

Read those pages before you fly. Notice what they have in common: almost every warning is about large cities, especially at night.

## Is Brazil getting more or less popular with tourists?

More. Brazil passed nine million international arrivals in 2025 for the first time, about 40% more than in 2024, according to Embratur figures. The United States was the third-largest source market, with nearly 678,000 visitors from January to November. Record numbers don't make a place risk-free, but they show that millions of people travel here every year without trouble.

## What are the real risks in Brazil?

**Phone theft.** This is the number one problem. The UK notes that thieves target phones especially between 4 pm and 9 pm, on buses, trains and sidewalks. Don't walk while texting, and use your phone inside a shop or café.

**Beach snatching.** On busy city beaches, groups sometimes run through grabbing bags. Bring only what you need: a little cash, a card, sunscreen.

**Drink spiking and dating apps.** The US advisory calls this "common, especially in Rio de Janeiro." Criminals meet foreigners on dating apps or at bars, drug them and empty their bank accounts. Buy your own drinks, watch them being poured, and meet new people in public places.

**A rare methanol scare.** In late 2025, a wave of spirits contaminated with methanol, concentrated in São Paulo state, made national headlines and led to a crackdown on illegal distilleries. Cases like this are not common in Brazil, which is exactly why it caused so much alarm, and it's why some foreign advisories still mention it. The precaution is simple: buy spirits only at established bars, restaurants and supermarkets, and skip very cheap bottles from brands you don't recognize.

**Getting around: use Uber or licensed taxis.** Uber and the local app 99 are widely used, affordable and reliable in Brazilian cities. Licensed taxis are just as safe: take them from the official booths at airports or call one through an app. Turn down drivers who approach you offering a ride, and check that the plate matches the one in the app.

**Cards and card machines.** Withdraw cash from ATMs inside banks or shopping malls, during the day, and cover the keypad when you type your PIN. On the beach and at small local businesses, pay in cash whenever you can: carry small notes for coconut water, mate, corn on the cob and chair and umbrella rentals. If you do pay by card, check the amount on the screen before you tap or enter your PIN, and never let your card out of your sight. In Rio, police have arrested beach vendors who charged foreign tourists absurd amounts on card machines.

## Is Rio de Janeiro safe for tourists?

Rio is where most safety questions start, and it rewards a little planning. Most visitors stay in the South Zone, in Copacabana, Ipanema, Leblon and Botafogo, neighborhoods with good hotels, restaurants and people on the streets until late. Enjoy the beaches by day. At night the beachfront promenade stays busy, but the sand empties out, so don't walk on it after dark. For longer distances at night, take an Uber or a licensed taxi.

Be especially careful with your phone in the busiest spots: the promenade, Arpoador at sunset, Lapa at night and the area around Maracanã on match days.

### Should you visit a favela?

**Only visit a favela (or "community," as locals say) with a local guide, never on your own.** Follow that rule and it's well worth it. The US and UK advisories recommend against visiting favelas, even on a guided tour, and you should know that before you decide. As locals, we think walking through a community with a guide who lives there is one of the most memorable things you can do in Rio. It's where you really understand how the city lives.

Our pick is Santa Marta, in Botafogo. Tours are led by guides who were born in the community. You ride the funicular to the top of the hill, where you get one of the best views in Rio, and visit the Michael Jackson spot and residents' social projects along the way.

On the tour:

- Go during the day and stay with your guide and group.
- Ask your guide before photographing people.
- If your guide says it's not a good day to go up, listen.

If something does happen anywhere in the city, Rio has a tourist police station, the DEAT (Delegacia Especial de Apoio ao Turismo), used to helping foreigners file reports.

## Which places in Brazil are safest for tourists?

::photo noronha

The places on most bucket lists are also the calmest. In Bonito, Fernando de Noronha, the Lençóis Maranhenses, Jalapão and the Pantanal, your concerns are mostly natural ones: strong sun, heat, currents and remote roads. Noronha is a small, controlled-access island where everyone seems to know everyone.

The national parks at Iguazu Falls and the Pantanal are specifically excluded from the US "Do not travel" border zone. In those places, the bigger risks are dehydration and slippery trails.

## Is Brazil safe for solo female travelers?

Many women travel here alone and love it. The same rules apply, with extra attention to drinks, dating apps and late-night transport. Share your plans with someone at home, take Uber or a licensed taxi at night, and choose small, well-reviewed hotels in central areas.

## Our simple safety checklist

- Leave your passport in the hotel safe and carry a photo of it.
- Carry a "day wallet" with a small amount of cash and one card.
- Keep your phone out of sight on the street.
- Don't wander into areas you don't know. Before you go out, check which parts of the city call for extra care, and ask your hotel or guide if you're unsure. Once you're out, read the street the way locals do: if nobody else is walking in a direction, don't go that way. Busy, well-lit streets are your best guide.
- Use Uber, 99 or licensed taxis, especially at night.
- Save the emergency numbers: 190 (police), 192 (ambulance), 193 (fire brigade).
- Buy travel insurance that covers theft and medical care.

## FAQ

**Is Brazil safe to visit right now?** Yes, for most travelers. The US rates Brazil Level 2 and the UK and Canada advise caution, mainly about urban crime. Avoid border areas, visit favelas only with a local guide, and follow basic city precautions.

**Is Rio de Janeiro safe at night?** Busy streets in Copacabana, Ipanema and Leblon are lively at night, but take an Uber or a licensed taxi instead of walking long distances and stay off the beach after dark.

**Can I drink the tap water in Brazil?** Most travelers stick to bottled or filtered water, which is cheap and available everywhere.

**Should I avoid wearing jewelry in Brazil?** Leave expensive watches and jewelry at home. Brazilians dress casually, and blending in is the easiest form of protection.

**Is Brazil safe for LGBTQ+ travelers?** Same-sex marriage has been legal nationwide since 2013, and Rio and São Paulo have large, visible LGBTQ+ scenes. Use the same caution as everyone else at night.
